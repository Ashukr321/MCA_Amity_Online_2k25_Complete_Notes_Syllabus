# 9. Recommendations & Conclusion

## 9.1 Recommendations

1. **Treat AI screening as decision support, not decision-making.** The literature shows organizations
   already trust AI more for content generation than for autonomous candidate filtering (Section 7.2);
   design and adopt AI recruitment tools that produce a ranked shortlist for human review rather than
   an automatic accept/reject decision.
2. **Keep scoring methodology transparent to recruiters.** Surface individual score components
   (skills, experience, semantic fit, nice-to-have) rather than a single combined percentage, to
   preserve the trust advantage identified in Section 8.2 as such tools scale.
3. **Build in security and cost governance from the outset.** Role-based access control, rate
   limiting, and AI-usage tracking with a non-AI fallback should be treated as baseline requirements
   for any platform handling personal candidate data and paid AI API calls, not later additions
   (Section 8.4).
4. **Formalize a periodic bias-review process, modeled on existing regulation.** Given documented
   cases (Section 5.6), peer-reviewed evidence of measurable bias (Section 5.7), and binding
   precedent such as NYC Local Law 144's independent annual audit requirement (Section 5.8),
   organizations should audit AI screening output on a recurring basis, not only at initial
   deployment — treating this as compliance-adjacent, not merely aspirational.
5. **Monitor adoption against outcome data, not adoption rate alone.** The 19% of adopters reporting
   missed qualified applicants (Section 7.1) shows that adoption rate is not itself a measure of
   quality; track false-negative screening outcomes explicitly.

## 9.2 Conclusion

This study set out to examine, through secondary research, the industry trend of applying Artificial
Intelligence to recruitment. The findings support the conclusion that AI-assisted resume screening is
a practical and rapidly growing response to a genuine scaling problem in recruitment — manual review
does not keep pace with application volume — and that responsible implementations treat AI as
decision-support within a structured, human-reviewed workflow rather than as an autonomous decision
system.

The evidence gathered here — adoption statistics, use-case distribution, a representative weighted
scoring methodology, and documented bias cases — points to three consistent themes across the
literature: AI adoption in recruiting is accelerating quickly; transparent, multi-factor scoring is
preferred over opaque single-number matching; and cost governance and bias mitigation must be
designed in deliberately rather than assumed. SkillMatch AI was used throughout as an illustrative
example of how these themes translate into a concrete system design, but the report's conclusions are
grounded in the broader secondary literature (SHRM, Gartner, and documented bias case studies) rather
than in any single implementation.

Overall, the project confirms the value of examining "AI in recruitment" through both quantitative
industry data and documented real-world outcomes: doing so surfaces not only the clear business case
for AI-assisted screening, but also the specific safeguards — transparency, human oversight, cost
control, and active bias review — that responsible adoption requires.
